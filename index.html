Bayesian Continuation MPC for Spacecraft Acquisition
Under Orbital Uncertainty

Carlo Schreiber1, Duncan Eddy1, Mahdi Al-Husseini1, Derek Woods2, Araz Feyzi2, Mykel J. Kochenderfer1

1 Stanford University2 Kayhan Space

Code BibTeX

Abstract

This paper introduces Bayesian Continuation Model Predictive Control (BC-MPC), an adaptive method for finding and establishing contact with spacecraft immediately after launch when their true positions are most uncertain. Launch and deployment errors can leave a spacecraft sufficiently far from its predicted position that operators must actively search during ground contacts. Each missed detection provides information about which orbital hypotheses remain plausible, but searching one part of the uncertainty region can consume the time needed to reach another. BC-MPC updates a particle-based orbital belief after missed detections and replans antenna pointing while retaining a feasible continuation. Across 2,773 attempts from 96 simulated deployments with matched uncertainty, BC-MPC improves acquisition probability over an Along-Track Uncertainty Sweep (ATUS) by 6.9 percentage points and reduces restricted mean acquisition time by 11.5 seconds. We additionally evaluate the method using pre- and post-deployment ephemeris for 83 spacecraft from the Transporter-16 rideshare launch. BC-MPC improves modeled acquisition over ATUS by approximately 12 percentage points under the common assumed uncertainty model.

The problem

Launch and early-orbit operations require operators to establish contact, assess spacecraft health, and transition toward routine communications despite uncertainty in the spacecraft’s position. Launch-vehicle injection errors, deployment dispersion, and atmospheric drag can produce uncertainty that exceeds the antenna beamwidth. Operators must therefore search around the predicted trajectory within limited ground contacts, respecting antenna slew, settling, and receiver timing constraints.

Bayesian Continuation MPC

BC-MPC maintains a particle-based belief over possible spacecraft trajectories and updates this belief after missed detections. If a pointing should have produced a detection for a particular orbit hypothesis, a miss reduces that hypothesis’s weight. Antenna motion constraints determine which search actions remain reachable later in the contact.

Starting from a feasible ATUS schedule, BC-MPC reoptimizes the next four antenna pointings while retaining the continuation beyond the planning horizon. Candidate pointings are evaluated together with this continuation over the full remaining contact window. The complete schedule must satisfy antenna motion constraints, including the transition to the retained continuation. Only the first pointing is executed. After a miss, the belief is updated, the optimized schedule is shifted by one stage, and the optimization is repeated.

Results

Single-spacecraft search

We evaluate first-contact acquisition across 2,773 attempts from 96 simulated deployment realizations near an orbit with an altitude of 550 km and an inclination of 97.6°. BC-MPC is initialized from an ATUS schedule with a planning horizon of four dwells and compared against a uniform grid sweep, ATUS, Bayesian greedy, and MPC without continuation. All controllers use a common 192-second search window, matched orbital uncertainty, and antenna motion constraints.

Fleet acquisition after launch

We investigate whether contact-level improvements translate to improved fleet-scale acquisition. Four simulated deployments each release 100 spacecraft, creating competing acquisition demands across a limited ground-station network. We measure the expected fraction acquired within the first 24 hours as the network expands from one to 24 sites, with four independently steerable antennas per site.

With six sites, the expected fraction acquired within 24 hours increases from 89.5% with ATUS to 94.2% with BC-MPC. The advantage is largest when ground-station opportunities are limited, where a missed acquisition is more difficult to recover from. As the network expands, additional contact opportunities reduce the impact of individual misses and the performance gap narrows. Both controllers approach 99% acquisition for the largest tested network.

These four deployments are separate from the single-spacecraft study. Sites are added in a fixed geographic order. The result is an expected acquired fraction, not the probability of acquiring every spacecraft.

Transporter-16 replay

We evaluate BC-MPC using operational orbit data for 83 spacecraft from the Transporter-16 rideshare launch, supplied by Kayhan Space. The dataset contains pre-deployment ephemeris distributed to operators three days before launch, post-deployment ephemeris, and the first cataloged two-line element set (TLE) correlated to each deployment. The pre- and post-deployment ephemeris represent the orbit products used operationally to cue early ground contacts. The first-catalog TLE is used only retrospectively as an approximate trajectory reference and is unavailable to the controllers.

We evaluate two planning cases, using either pre- or post-deployment ephemeris as controller input, with the same six ground stations and search configuration used in the single-spacecraft evaluation. Both cases yield similar results: BC-MPC improves modeled acquisition over ATUS by approximately 12 percentage points while also reducing restricted mean acquisition time (RMST). The improvement observed in the synthetic evaluation is retained when using operational orbit products.
